# Offers lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **Low effort for the buyer.** Once the bottle arrives, refills are automatic and renewals ship free. The ongoing work is close to zero, which is the side of the value equation most offers get wrong.
- **Healthy unit margins on paper.** 47-61% after direct costs, and the plan is cheap to run: about US$120 a month fixed and a Shopify store already live. The downside is small. Stock spend is ~US$10,480.
- **The single-filter plan is something LARQ does not sell.** The buyer replaces filters on time without stocking up, and that is a real, cheap-to-deliver part of the stack.
- **The plan has a margin buffer.** Net profit of ~$20,970 plus the $15,000 ad budget leaves ~$35,970 before ads. Ads could cost about **US$36 per bottle** before year 1 breaks even. That is 2.4x the assumed $15.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **It competes on price, so it is a commodity.** The only stated difference from LARQ is being ~23% cheaper on the bottle and ~7% cheaper on the 3-pack plan. It is level on the one-time 3-pack. There is no stated dream outcome (taste? health? never buying plastic?), no defined buyer and no reason beyond price to pick Claro. Brita, Water-to-Go and others are unmapped and may be cheaper still. The lens asks whether you could double the price and still be the obvious choice. Today the answer is no. The bottle would sit at ~$80 against LARQ's $54.95-65.
2. **Likelihood is unproven, and the claims carry risk.** "150 L" comes from the supplier's own test, with no lab report, and filter performance and water-quality claims are not certified. A filter brand with no proof that it filters fails on perceived likelihood. It also risks refunds, platform ad rejections and regulatory trouble in six markets. There is no guarantee to offset this.
3. **The market is weak on pain and reach, and growth is unknown.** A reusable bottle solves a mild pain. 72% of the forecast sales are bottle-only, so the recurring revenue that justifies the model is a minority. Expected single-plan renewals at 70% retention are 0.7/0.3 ≈ 2.3, worth about $16.80 of profit per subscriber. The $15/bottle ad cost, the 25% creator-code share and every retention rate are assumptions. Market size and real CAC are open questions: **/founder-competitors** for the competitive set and market size, **/founder-consumer** for buy rates and price tolerance, and **/founder-cfo** to stress-test CAC and retention.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Who exactly is the buyer (age, situation, why now) and what result do they want? Better taste, safer tap water, travel, less plastic?
- Why would a buyer believe the filter works? What proof can you show by launch?
- Is the bottle insulated? If not, how does it stand against LARQ's insulated bottle beyond price?
- What guarantee can the margins afford? For example, a refund if they do not prefer the taste in 30 days.
- What do creators actually ask for (flat fees, free product, commission rate), and what has one test campaign cost per bottle?
- Why do 72% of buyers skip the plan, and what would make the plan the default?
- What is EU delivery time, and what does a 4-8 day wait do to conversion?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Proof before scale.** Get an independent lab report on filter capacity and what the filter removes before any filtration claim runs in ads. Without it the likelihood score stays near zero.
2. **An offer, not a discount.** Define one target buyer and their dream outcome. Then build a stack around it: the auto-refill plan, a named guarantee the CFO signs off, and real bonuses that cost little to deliver. Test whether it holds at or above LARQ's price (**/founder-offer**, **/founder-pricing**).
3. **Real numbers replace the assumptions.** Validate demand and plan take-up with **/founder-consumer**. Then run a paid test on the first ~100 bottles. Proceed only if blended acquisition cost stays well under the ~$36/bottle break-even and the share choosing a plan beats 28%.
